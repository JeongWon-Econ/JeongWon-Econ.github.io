Moving Opportunities? South Korea's Elite High School Relocation (1974–1987) (CEP Fund for Early Career Independent Research Projects)

Across societies, students from high-SES backgrounds are often overrepresented in a small set of established schools that disproportionately produce elite positions. To what extent do families value access to such selective schools, and how important is such access for sustaining intergenerational persistence? To answer these questions, I leverage a natural experiment that reshuffled access to elite schools in South Korea (1976-1984), when authoritarian governments forcibly relocated elite high schools from Seoul’s old urban core to then-sparsely populated rural outskirts. I first present evidence that families highly valued access to elite schools and that access became rapidly stratified by family background. I then use birth-cohort variation among origin-native residents to estimate the effects of losing access on upward mobility outcomes.

Intergenerational Transmission of Elite Status in Meritocracy: Joseon Korea (15-19C) (with Misha Seong)

How does class shape the intergenerational transmission of elite status under meritocracy? We study this question in the historical context of Joseon Korea (15th-19th), where elite status was predominantly attained through competitive civil service examinations for nearly five centuries. By tracing both the ancestors and descendants of elite bureaucrats, we document substantial, persistent class gaps in elite transmission among individuals who attained the same elite status through the same examination. We then investigate the mechanisms underlying these disparities, including networks, ability, cultural capital, and other sources of advantage.
