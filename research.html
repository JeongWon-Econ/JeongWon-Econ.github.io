Reshuffling Access to Elite Schools: Forced Relocation of Elite High Schools in South Korea (1976-1984) (CEP Fund for Early Career Independent Research Projects)

Across societies, elite schools are often perceived as an early channel for elite transmission, yet we know little about how policies can reduce access gaps and how much these schools reinforce advantages across generations. This paper asks three relevant questions: if access to elite schools were reshuffled on a large scale, would families continue to value access to them? Would access gaps remain reduced, or widen again? And to what extent would incumbents lose from, and adapt to, the shock? To answer these questions, I leverage a large-scale reshuffling policy in South Korea (1976-1987). Following district-based admission reforms, elite schools were forcibly relocated from Seoul's old urban core to then-sparsely populated rural outskirts. First, I document a “gold-rush” response to the school relocations: announcements of elite school moves triggered sharp population inflows in receiving neighbourhoods. Second, I show that access to elite schools quickly became uneven: within five years, the parental college-graduate share in receiving districts rose by 22 percentage points. Third, I use birth-cohort variation among native residents of the origin district to estimate the effects of losing elite school access on upward mobility. Using Population Census microdata, I find that educational attainment falls sharply for the first cohort exposed to each relocation wave. Effects align more closely with prestige-weighted measures of school departures than with simple counts of relocated schools.

Intergenerational Transmission of Elite Status in Meritocracy: Joseon Korea (15-19C) (with Misha Seong)

How does class shape the intergenerational transmission of elite status under meritocracy? We study this question in the historical context of Joseon Korea (15th-19th), where elite status was predominantly attained through competitive civil service examinations for nearly five centuries. By tracing both the ancestors and descendants of elite bureaucrats, we document substantial, persistent class gaps in elite transmission among individuals who attained the same elite status through the same examination. We then investigate the mechanisms underlying these disparities, including networks, ability, cultural capital, and other sources of advantage.
